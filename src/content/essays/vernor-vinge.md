I do not remember when I first encountered Vernor Vinge. It might have been after I came across a discussion on his [essay on technological singularity](https://edoras.sdsu.edu/~vinge/misc/singularity.html) on one of the Usenet science fiction groups back in the 90s.

I found that essay, read it, and I was hooked. This was before singularity was derisively called the rapture of nerds. I might have been one of those nerds who proselytized the wonders of singularity to my “civilian” friends.

I think the first book that I read by him was his collection of short stories called *True Names ... and Other Dangers*. The idea that you could create a full-sensorium virtual experience with low bandwidth data connection because most of the work would be done by your own brain is fascinating to me even today. And in the world in which catfishing and fishing has become such a problem the power of true names is something that we see everyday. The novela [*True Names*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Names) is a real classic.

I think after that I came across the *Across Real Time* trilogy. This is a classic science fiction story. It extrapolates from known physics to the invention of "bobbles" \- bubbles that manifest physically in our universe but within them the time does not advance. The first novel, [*The Peace War*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Peace_War)*,* is still one of my favorite books. Vinge tells a brilliant story of the apocalypse brought on by this technology but also the resilience of a distributed band of smart hackers against a monolithic world government with characters that you really care and root for. I particularly enjoyed it because large parts of the novel take place in the Bay Area, specifically Livermore and the East Bay. The novel [*Marooned In Realtime*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marooned_in_Realtime) is a detective story based in the far future in which versions of the characters in Peace War reappear. It is not as much fun as Peace War, but a great read nonetheless.

I must have read his collection of short stories called [*The Collected Stories of Vernor Vinge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Collected_Stories_of_Vernor_Vinge) sometime in the early aughts. The novela called [*The Ungoverned*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ungoverned) blew my socks off. I was just becoming aware of the politics of gun control in America and I was blown away by this story that sort of sketches the logical conclusion of one of the pro-gun arguments \- I need guns so that I can resist the government. It plays out as a confrontation between a recalcitrant farmer who has his own nuke and a budding post-apocalyptic nation state which has tanks, planes and other hardware. The whole concept of privatized justice and privatized law enforcement in this story also tickled my fancy. There are many gems in this short story collection like [*Fast Times at Fairmont High*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Times_at_Fairmont_High), a brilliant novela which showed us what a true augmented-reality-based world would look like 25 years ago. Again, like everything else that he wrote, it had many brilliant scientific ideas. Like smart lab mice who have escaped from a biotechnology company who seem to have developed their own little civilization. I used to chaperone the kids from my [children's choir](https://www.vivaceyouthchorus.org/) on a yearly retreat [at a farm](https://www.hiddenvilla.org/) near where we live. I used to always be given the cabin with the most rambunctious preteens. I have calmed them down with the promise of and put them to sleep by reading stories from this collection many a night. Good times.

Sometime in the 90s Vinge asked himself the question \- “If singularity is a point after which what came before is no guide for what is going to come after, and I postulate that this will happen in the next few decades, then as a science fiction writer I have painted myself in a corner. How can I write convincingly about what happens in the future?”. As a solution he came up with the concept of Zones of Thought, where the closer you are to the core of the Galaxy the lower the probability that your automation would become sentient. So, further out you have these post singularity civilizations which are almost godlike but which also flower and wither away in a blink of an eye and closer to the center of the Galaxy which is approximately where the Earth is, you have civilizations where the singularity does not happen. It is a continuum of course and it is possible to travel from one zone to another. Vinge wrote three space opera novels ([*A Fire Upon the Deep*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fire_Upon_the_Deep), [*A Deepness in the Sky*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Deepness_in_the_Sky) , [*The Children of the Sky*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Children_of_the_Sky)) based in this universe which are utterly fascinating.

The very first chapter of *A Fire Upon the Deep* describes a desperate effort by some human colonists in the Far Beyond (the region of space where it is super easy for automation to become sentient), who, in their hubris have awoken ancient sentient automation that is "evil". The way the automation takes control of the ship which is trying to get off to hyperspace, running completely silent, with no inbound packets being accepted using some atmospheric sensors on the skin of the spaceship was one of the best beginnings of any book I have read. It was utterly fascinating and heart-wrenching. The two extra-terrestrial species that he imagines, and the Queng Ho trading armada of human ships and the long lived human characters (because of deep sleep) are all three dimensional, believable, and just a lot of fun (I think Adrian Tchaikovsky’s *The Children of Time* series is a worthy successor to these novels, but I plan to write more on that later). The mind control technology where the memory and emotions of a human are completely suppressed but the cognitive brain is left functioning and the training and education fully accessible so that he or she can be used as a fully compliant slave was horrifying. Also the idea of a fog of nano sensors occupying a volume to provide all kinds of data about it was brilliant. And these are just the ones I remember off-hand.

Even today I go back and reread many of these books and I relive some of the excitement that I felt when I read them first. Vernor Vinge died on March 20, 2024\. Every time I went to a bookshop I would hopefully check the SF shelves to see if there was a new Vinge novel. We will not get a new novel from him any longer.
