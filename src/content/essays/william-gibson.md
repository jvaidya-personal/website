I am trying to remember how I was introduced to William Gibson. I think it was my friend John when I was working at Informix. John was an old-style Unix programmer, but was also a bit of a dandy. He lived in San Francisco, dressed well, had a thinning ponytail and I'm reasonably certain, had a girlfriend and not a boyfriend. I was an FOB (fresh off the boat) then and he must have found my enthusiasm for science fiction, expressed in, what I am sure then was a thick Indian accent, somewhat amusing. I think he was the one who first told me about the Neuromancer by William Gibson. As an aside, John also introduced me to the National Public Radio and the local Bay area station KQED 88.5 FM, of which I became a listener and a loyal supporter.

But once I bought Neuromancer, it must have been from A Clean Well Lit Place for Books in downtown Menlo Park, which is where the Informix campus was, I could not get enough of William Gibson.

Once I started reading Neuromancer, it was love from the very first page. Lustrous sentences, the laconic but still lovable characters, and fierce, take no bullshit from nobody heroines all set in an imagined cyberpunk future that you were immersed into without any explanation at all. Other futuristic science fiction that I had read until then was basically written for somebody from our era \- with explanations for the technologies that were being used or about people's professions or about the social milieu in this imagined future. In contrast, Gibson's novels read like they were written for somebody who lived in this imagined future. There were no explanations. With the first iconic sentence of the Neuromancer, “The sky above the port was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel”, you were plunged into a world of Chiba City in Japan, the Sprawl on the East Coast of United States (also called the BAMA or the Boston Atlanta Metropolitan Area), the Fusion Authority and companies like Hosaka, Ono Sendai, Zeiss Nikon and Maas Biolabs Gmbh. You ate up the descriptions of the top-end computer gear made by extremely plausible sounding but still futuristic companies like Ono Sendai (which made the equivalent of a powerful desktop) and Zeiss Nikon (virtual reality contact lenses/implants) and you wanted to live in that world. As I said, it was love at first read.

After that I acquired and devoured all his available books like a glutton. I read his Sprawl trilogy ([*Neuromancer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuromancer)*, [Count Zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Zero), and [Mona Lisa Overdrive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa_Overdrive)*), then the gem-like stories in [*Burning Chrome*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_Chrome_\(short_story_collection\)). *Burning Chrome* is the title story. It has beautiful moody descriptions of the bleak weather and even bleaker sprawlscape, the supercool cyber raid on Mafia computers systems when they "burn" Chrome (a Mafia figurehead and Madame) and "meat-puppets" \- women who have neural shunts installed so that they can participate in prostitution without any conscious memory of it \- a chilling prediction on what hyper capitalism would bring forth given the existence of the technology. This is the world of the regulars at The Gentleman Loser: Bobby Quine, Automatic Jack, Johnny Mnemonic, Molly Millions aka Sally Shears and the tragic (or was she?) Rikki. The iconic Molly in Neuromancer was the inspiration for Trinity in the Matrix movies. All these things in the Sprawl  made permanent homes in my mind.

It did not take me too long to realize that Gibson was indeed a gifted writer, but what he was writing was most definitely genre fiction. His heroes, who were quite compromised on the surface, nonetheless had a heart of gold. His heroines who are tough and who knew how to make their way in the unfair world on their own, thank you very much, like Molly who is augmented to have retractable razors for nails (“razorgirl”) and who does not flinch at skinning alive a perverted US Senator, still has an honor code and a heart of gold. The billionaires and monomaniacal (possibly on Asperger's spectrum) billionaires are all described as almost belonging to a different species who marched to their own drums and also to some inscrutable logic of the post-capitalist markets. But although these characters were not as full-fledged as you would want them, that did not mean that you did not care for them, because you did, in spades. And this, combined with a superb imaginative grasp of the kind of world and economy and systems that high technology would bring forth and the language that glittered like polished diamonds made my love affair with him continue and it has continued until today.

After The Sprawl came the Bridge trilogy ([*Virtual Light*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_Light)*, [Idoru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idoru), [All Tomorrow's Parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Tomorrow%27s_Parties)*). This one brought us another tough heroine in bicycle leathers and the cool half destroyed bay bridge with its huge homeless / marginal people encampment. And Rydell the rent-a-cop with a heart of gold.

Around the year 2000, in the midst of the first internet boom, as much of the future that Gibson had predicted back in the mid 80s actually commenced to present itself, Gibson started a new series of novels. The attacks on the World Trade Center on September 11th 2001, the incompetence and corruption with which the Iraq war was executed by the second Bush administration deeply shook William Gibson like many of us. He came out with the Blue Ant (Blue Ant is an enigmatic holding company that features in all three novels and is run by a billionaire named Huebertus Bigend. Yes, :)) trilogy with novels ([*Pattern Recognition*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_Recognition_\(novel\))*, [Spook Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spook_Country), [Zero History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_History)*) that are set in mostly contemporary times that I still love to go back to.

His response to the Trump years was *[The Peripheral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Peripheral)* and [*The Agency*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agency_\(novel\)) which are novels that take place in two timelines one is in the near future (20-25ish years in the future)and the other is seventy years in the future from the first timeline. This is Gibson's attempt at tackling time travel and he does it effortlessly. There is very little hand waving that we have to believe and the possibility of time travel paradox is handled neatly. He again gave us a tough-as-nails heroine from rural Appalachia in Flynne Fisher (the names of heroines in his books are always memorable, the other one that comes to my mind is Casey Pollard).

People have tried to bring Gibson's books to screen with varying success. But [The Peripheral on Amazon Prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Peripheral_\(TV_series\)) is worth a watch. His characters are faithfully recreated and so is the texture of the world in the near future \- on the cusp of a general environmental and political breakdown euphemistically called The Jackpot \- and then seventy years later when the human population has drastically gone down, biodiversity been decimated, but the technology has continued its march and the world is ruled by kleptocrats. Sadly the series was not renewed after two seasons, I wish it had been.

Every few years a new William Gibson book comes out,  and he's one of the few authors whose books I buy hardcover.  I know I will be rereading them and I think that they're worth spending my shelf space on.
